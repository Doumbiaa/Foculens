FocusLens · A computer vision study

Computer Vision, with an Eye Toward Human Perception

Can a webcam tell when you have stopped studying?

We built FocusLens, a Pomodoro timer that pauses when you look away, recorded 12 students to test it, and measured what a camera can and cannot see of attention.

The short answer

Partly. For lapses that change how your head or eyes look, such as a phone in your lap, a turn toward a friend, or closed eyes, a webcam can tell. Our rule-based detector paused for 83% of lapses lasting five seconds or more, and a small neural network labelled 86.6% of frames correctly for two people it had never seen. For lapses that happen inside the head, such as thinking with your eyes on the screen, it cannot. It also pauses wrongly: 0.42 times per minute of real focus on our recordings, about ten times our own target. How often live users see wrong pauses is pending: about 10 testers.

Alice's 25 minutes

Meet Alice, a fictional AI for Science student at AIMS South Africa. We invented her to make the problem concrete, and every number about her night is made up for illustration. It is two in the morning and she has set a 25-minute Pomodoro timer11. She spends 8 minutes on her phone, 3 minutes talking to a friend beside her, and 5 minutes lost in thought. She studies for the other 9. The timer reaches 00:00 and congratulates her on a full Pomodoro.

Drag the slider. The timer only knows how much clock time has passed. It has no idea what Alice was doing.

FocusLens is our attempt to fix this. It watches the webcam, and when Alice stops looking at her screen it pauses the timer and says why. Everything runs in the browser, so no video leaves her laptop. This post asks whether that idea works, and where it breaks.

The question

Can a computer vision system, using only an ordinary laptop webcam, tell when someone is focused on their screen, well enough to pause a study timer for a person it has never seen?

Why this is hard

Focus happens inside a head, and a webcam sees only the outside of it. So we split lapses into two kinds. A visible lapse changes how the head or eyes look: turning away, looking down, closing the eyes, leaving the chair. An invisible lapse leaves them unchanged: staring at the screen while thinking about dinner. A camera can only ever work on the first kind, and any score we report is a score for visible focus.

There is a second difficulty, and it matters more for a timer than accuracy does. A timer that pauses wrongly gets switched off. Telling a real lapse from a blink, a glance at your notes, or deep concentration is the hard part.

What was already known

Pausing something when you look away is not new. The eyeLook phone paused a video whenever its eye-contact sensor saw the viewer look elsewhere1. The authors described prototypes and use scenarios rather than a formal evaluation.

Several recent projects build webcam focus monitors for students. Jamunarani and colleagues combine MediaPipe face landmarks, gaze, head pose and eye openness, and wait several seconds of diverted gaze before alerting. They ran a 10-person, hour-long study and report focus rising from about 60% to 85%, without a described statistical test2. Nagpure and colleagues describe rules based on eye aspect ratio5 below 0.25 for two seconds and head angle beyond 25 degrees. Their results table is labelled "expected results", and no experiments are reported3. FocusGuard, a public project, extracts 17 features with MediaPipe and trains a small MLP and XGBoost on 9 participants. It reports a leave-one-person-out F1 of about 0.86 and a 12-point drop from a random split to that stricter split4.

What we did not find in any of them is how often the timer pauses when the person is actually focusing, measured on someone the system has not seen. That is the number a Pomodoro user feels, so it is the number we report.

What we expected

Our proposal, written before we collected data, set a target and predicted the failures. The target was a median of at most 0.04 false pauses per minute on held-out people, about one wrong pause per 25-minute session. We predicted three failure modes: downward gaze mistaken for closed eyes, reading or deep thought mistaken for distraction, and tracking loss in poor light. We also expected the visible lapses to be the easy ones. Section What we found shows how each of these came out.

What a webcam sees of a lapse

MediaPipe's face landmarker finds 478 points on a face in each frame and estimates head rotation and 52 blendshape scores6. We use only a few numbers from that: how far the head is turned (yaw) and tilted (pitch), measured from where the person normally sits, and how closed each eye is (a blink score from 0 to 1). Try them below. The thresholds are the ones in the app.

A rule alone would pause on every blink and every glance at a notebook. So the timer waits. Anything under half a second is ignored, and then the person gets a grace period, 5 seconds by default, before the timer pauses. It resumes 0.3 seconds after they are back in range. The simulator below runs those rules over a made-up version of Alice's night so you can see the trade-off. The grace period and the angle limit pull in opposite directions.

How we tested it

This section is long on purpose, so that someone else could redo the work. Figure 4 shows the whole system. The rest of the section covers the data, the labels, the models and the metric, in that order.

The 13 features

Each frame becomes 13 numbers. Pose is measured relative to the person's own 3-second calibration, so a laptop on a lap desk or a tilted screen does not matter. Appendix A has the full table. In short: yaw, pitch, roll, their absolute values, the combined angle pose_magnitude = √(yaw² + pitch²), both blink scores and their maximum, a one-second rolling mean of that maximum (to tell a blink from a nap), a downward-gaze score and a sideways-gaze score.

Detector 1: rules

The rule detector uses no learning. Yaw or pitch beyond 15° (strict), 20° or 25° is a violation. The worse eye decides: a blink score of 0.5 or more counts as closed. A 2° hysteresis stops it flickering at the limit, and the newest frame gets half the weight in smoothing. A Notes mode lets the head tilt down up to 40°. When several things are wrong at once, the reported reason follows a fixed order: no face, eyes, left, right, down, up.

Detector 2: a small neural network

The second detector is an MLP, a neural network with one hidden layer, that takes the 13 features and outputs focused or lost focus. We chose its settings with Optuna8, using 30 trials scored by the same person-held-out cross-validation described below. The search covered the hidden layer layout (64; 64 and 32; 128 and 64 units), the activation (ReLU or tanh), the L2 penalty (0.001 to 1) and the starting learning rate (0.001 to 0.01). The winner:

Pipeline([
  ("scaler", StandardScaler()),
  ("clf", MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64,), activation="relu", solver="adam",
          alpha=0.6009504, learning_rate_init=0.0020153, max_iter=300,
          early_stopping=True, random_state=42))])   # scikit-learn 1.6.1, Adam, stopped after 61 epochs

The large L2 penalty is the notable choice. It keeps the network small in effect and, as Figure 8 shows, it cut the gap between training and held-out scores.

Detector 3: both together

In the hybrid mode the MLP decides whether the person is focused, and the rules supply the reason (left, right, down, up, eyes closed), so that every pause explains itself. We did not evaluate the hybrid mode separately. Its verdict is the MLP's.

Recording 12 people

No public dataset matched our setting, so we recorded our own. Twelve participants (codes P01 to P06 and P09 to P14) each studied for about five minutes, reading a paper or learning to code, in a session with an annotator watching. Participants are identified only by code. confirm consent wording A collection tool, written for this project, logs the MediaPipe values for every frame along with the state of a key.

Each session starts with natural study. A scripted block of about 3 minutes follows, in which voice and text prompts on the screen ask the participant to do things like pick up a phone or turn to a neighbour. Scripted lapses give us enough lost-focus examples to learn from. The cost is that they are more obvious than most real lapses (see Where it fails). The tool also records optional self-reported age, gender and ethnicity in a separate file. With 12 people we did not analyse by group.

About 71,000 frames were recorded. After removing calibration, transitions, the first and last 10 seconds of each session, and ambiguous short presses, about 60,000 were usable. The model sees only frames in which a face was found, which leaves 51,017 labelled frames, of which 29.5% are lost-focus. The share of lost-focus frames was higher before that last filter (about 35%). Frames in which the person left the camera's view are probably the most extreme lapses, and the model never sees them. We did not verify the cause.

From key presses to labels

A person holding a key is a slow sensor. We measured the delay by comparing key events with the frames. The key goes down a median of 0.72 s after the participant really loses focus (session medians ranged from 0.25 s to 2.95 s), but comes up only 0.04 s after they return. So we shifted lapse starts back by 0.7 s and ends by 0.05 s. Shifting both by 0.8 s moved the ends too far.

Two more rules apply. A key press shorter than 2 seconds does not count as a lapse and its frames are masked (74 of the 246 presses were dropped, leaving 172). The first and last 10 seconds of each session are trimmed, because people were arriving and leaving: a face was found in only 51% of one participant's last 10 seconds. The final command:

python tools/load_session.py sessions/* --lag 0.7 --lag-end 0.05 \
       --trim-start 10 --trim-end 10 --short-press mask --save labelled.csv

Splitting by person

The split is by person, never by frame. Neighbouring frames of one person are nearly identical, and a random split lets a model memorise faces. FocusGuard's own README reports a 12-point drop when it switches to a person-held-out split4. We assigned 8 people to training, 2 to validation and 2 to a test set that we only used to score finished models. Cross-validation (4 folds, each fold holding out whole people) runs over the 10 training and validation people.

The metric

Because about 70% of frames are focused, accuracy alone flatters a lazy model. We report macro F1, the average of the F1 score for each class, so that missing the lost-focus class costs as much as missing the focused class. We also report accuracy, since it is easy to read, and compare against a model that does nothing but say "focused". For the rule detector, frame-level scores are misleading by design, since the first five seconds of every lapse are inside the grace period. We therefore count whole lapses: did the timer pause during a lapse of 5 seconds or more? We also count false pauses per minute of focus.

A second dataset

To see whether our result depends on our own recordings, we ran the same recipe on FocusGuard's public data4: 9 people, 144,793 frames, 5 features (yaw, pitch, roll and the eye aspect ratio5 of each eye). The split is 6 people for training, 2 for validation and 1 for test. Note that its label 1 means focused, the reverse of ours. This is a different set of people, labels, tasks and features, so the two sets of scores are not a ranking of datasets.

Live testing with users

Scores on recordings do not tell us what using the app feels like. So about 10 people who were not in the recordings use the deployed app for one-minute sessions while really studying, facing the screen, and count each time the timer pauses although they were still focusing. Each tester does this once per detector (rules, MLP, both). We report the count per minute for every tester and the median. Section The app shows where those numbers will appear.

What we found

Main finding

On two people the model had never seen, the neural network labelled 86.6% of frames correctly (macro F1 0.852), against 63.9% for a model that always answers "focused". It catches 77.5% of lost-focus frames, and 84% of the frames it calls lost-focus really are.

Beating the lazy baseline

The first boring explanation for a good score is that most people are focused most of the time. In the test set 63.9% of frames are focused, so answering "focused" every time would be right 63.9% of the time. All four of our tuned models clear that comfortably.

The second boring explanation is that the network has memorised its training people. Two checks point against it. The test people were never seen in training, and cross-validation over held-out people gives a similar score (0.827 ± 0.028 macro F1) to the test (0.852). Both numbers tell us the model generalises somewhat to new people. With only 12 people, "somewhat" is the strongest word the data supports.

Four models, tuned and untuned

We compared four model families: logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting and the MLP. We ran each untuned, then tuned. The dot plot shows three scores per model. The gap between the hollow circle (score on the training people) and the filled circle (cross-validated score on held-out people) is overfitting.

Three things stand out. First, tuning mostly fixed overfitting. The untuned MLP had a train-to-validation gap of 0.195, and tuning cut it to 0.066. Second, on our data the top three models are close. Their test F1 values (0.852, 0.846, 0.845) differ by less than 0.01 on only two people, so we call them comparable, not ranked. Logistic regression is clearly worse (0.811). We deployed the MLP: it also has the best cross-validated score. Note that we picked the champion using test F1, and the same model happens to lead in cross-validation too, so the choice would have been the same either way. Third, cross-validation scores after tuning are a little optimistic, because the tuning used the same folds. The test score is the clean one.

On FocusGuard's data the cross-validated scores land in the same range (about 0.81 for the best three models), but the test score is 0.96. That test is one person, and cross-validation is a better guide. We deployed the model trained on our data because it uses exactly the 13 features the browser computes, and because we know how the labels were made.

What the network looks at

We measured how much the test score drops when one feature's values are shuffled (permutation importance, 10 repeats, on the test people). We also show the Gini importance of a random forest, which asks a different question: which features did the trees split on?

The network leans on how far the head is from straight ahead (pose_magnitude), how closed the eyes are (blink_max and its rolling mean) and head pitch. That is what the rules use as well. The Gini view ranks yaw and abs_yaw higher than permutation does. The two views disagree because several features carry the same information: pose_magnitude contains yaw and pitch. Shuffling yaw alone barely hurts, since the network can read it from the others. A score near zero means redundant, not useless.

The rule detector on our recordings

We scored the rules at the level of lapses. Of 126 lapses lasting 5 seconds or more, the timer paused during 105 (83%). In natural study the rate was 31 of 35 (89%), and in the scripted block 74 of 91 (81%). The price was 0.42 false pauses per minute of real focus (14 in 33.1 minutes), and 0.60 per minute in natural study alone, the number a user would feel. Lapses shorter than the grace period are ignored by design, so they do not count as misses.

False pauses are not spread evenly. Two of the 12 sessions (P03 and P06) produced 11 of the 14, and both were annotated by the same person with many dropped short presses. We read that as a hint about label noise, not a finding.

Our proposal set a target of 0.04 false pauses per minute. The rule detector is far above it, at 0.42 pooled over all sessions. Pooled and median-per-person rates are not the same thing, so this is not a perfectly like-for-like comparison, but a gap of ten times is too large to be explained by that.

The app, and what its users say

FocusLens is a static web page in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, so it runs on any computer with a CPU and a webcam and is hosted on Render. It has a light and a dark theme, keeps working when the tab is minimised, and a sound warns you before the pause. You can change the grace window (3 to 30 s) and the strictness, switch between the three detectors, turn on Notes mode, blur your own video, recalibrate, and send feedback. Try it here.

Where it fails

These are the failures we know about. Some we measured, some we saw in the live app, and some follow from what the camera can and cannot see. We say which is which.

What it is for, and who it may fail

Meant for

  • One person, alone, choosing to use a study timer.
  • Sitting facing a screen.
  • Running on their own device, with nothing leaving it.

Not meant for

  • Proctoring, grading or checking up on someone.
  • Any case where another person sees the result.
  • Judging anyone's effort or ability.

May work worse for

  • People who think by looking away or pacing.
  • People who study on paper or on several screens.
  • People or settings unlike our 12 (face, skin tone, glasses glare, age, lighting). We did not test this.

The last point deserves to be said plainly. We recorded 12 people who are mostly classmates, and we have not measured whether the face tracker or our model works equally well across skin tones, ages and genders. With this sample we could not. A fair version of this system needs a more varied dataset and a per-group evaluation, and until then people unlike our participants should expect more wrong pauses. Pausing on head turns also penalises people whose attention does not look like attention. We would treat that as a design flaw, not as a user error.

What our setup cannot tell you

  • Not much about new people. There are 12 participants and only 2 test people. Cross-validated scores move by about ±0.03 depending on who is held out.
  • Not what "focused" really was. Labels are one classmate's reading of another person's attention, pressed about 0.7 s late, with short presses discarded.
  • Nothing about a whole night. Sessions are about five minutes. Fatigue and boredom after an hour are absent, and a large part of the lost-focus frames comes from a scripted block, where lapses are more obvious than natural ones.
  • Not the bias from dropped frames. Frames without a detected face are excluded from training, which probably removes the most extreme lapses.
  • Not whether landmarks beat pixels. Our proposal planned a pixel-based comparison model, a fine-tuned image classifier. We did not get to it, so we cannot say landmark features are the better choice.
  • Not how the hybrid mode does. We did not evaluate it separately.

What this means

For how models see

The model sees the angle of a head and the closure of two eyelids. It does not see attention. It works well while posture and attention move together and fails when they separate. This is true of every system of this kind, and thinking with your eyes on the screen is the clearest example. A confident-looking score of 0.85 F1 is a score for a proxy.

For how people see

A classmate sitting next to you would not mistake thinking for scrolling. They would use context: what is on the desk, how long it has lasted, whether you were just writing. Our model has none of that. It also cannot tell a phone in the lap from a notebook in the lap, which a person reads in an instant.

With another week

  • A thinking mode that tolerates looking up or away for longer, and a way to tell it apart from a phone.
  • Multi-face handling so that only the user's face counts.
  • Automatic notes detection, so that Notes mode does not need to be switched on by hand.
  • False pauses per minute on many more live users, with different faces, glasses and lighting, and a per-group breakdown.
  • A temporal model over a window of frames, and the pixel-based comparison from our proposal.

So, can a CV system tell when someone is focused? It can tell when they look unfocused. Whether that is good enough depends on what a wrong pause costs. For a study timer a wrong pause is a small annoyance, and the right pause is useful. For anything that judges a person, the answer is no.

E. Glossary

Macro F1
The F1 score (a balance of precision and recall) computed for each class and averaged, so a rare class counts as much as a common one.
Cross-validation by person
Splitting the data into folds so that all frames of a person fall in one fold. Each fold is scored by a model that never saw those people.
MLP
Multilayer perceptron, a small feed-forward neural network.
Blendshape
A 0 to 1 score from MediaPipe for one facial movement, such as eyeBlinkLeft.
EAR
Eye aspect ratio5, a ratio of eyelid opening to eye width computed from landmarks.
False pause
The timer pauses although the person was still focusing.
Grace period
How long the head or eyes may stay out of range before the timer pauses.
